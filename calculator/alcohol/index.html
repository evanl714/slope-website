Habit taper calculator / Alcohol

Plan more alcohol-free days.

“Drink less” is a hard goal to check at the end of a week. Slope makes it countable: whole days without a drink, with a little more room between drinking days each phase.

The calculator starts you at a 2:1 ratio over 60 days. That means two alcohol-free days, then one day when you may drink, then the pattern repeats. Each phase adds one more alcohol-free day to the run: three, then four, five, six, and a full seven in a row by the last week. Across the two months that comes to 49 alcohol-free days and 11 drinking days. Sixty days is long enough to cover a couple of birthdays, a few Fridays and at least one bad week at work, which is where the real test is.

Read this first. If your body has come to depend on alcohol, stopping or sharply cutting back can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Don’t use this calculator, and talk to a doctor before you change how much you drink, if any of these are true:

  • You drink every day or almost every day, or drink heavily most weeks.
  • You get shaky, sweaty, anxious, or sick, or can’t sleep, when you go without a drink.
  • You drink in the morning to feel steady.
  • You’ve had withdrawal symptoms, a withdrawal seizure, or delirium tremens before.

A clinician can help you cut back safely, sometimes with medication. In the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline is free, confidential, and open 24/7: 1-800-662-4357. The Slope app asks about physical dependence before it builds any plan, and won’t build one if you answer yes.

Two kinds of day, both with a number

This page is for people who drink a few evenings a week and would like more of their evenings to go without. The NIAAA’s Rethinking Drinking tips suggest deciding two things up front: how many days a week you want to drink, and how many drinks you’ll have on those days. They also say it’s a good idea to have some days when you don’t drink at all. The calculator handles the first question. You answer the second.

An off day is zero drinks. Not one small glass, not a taste of someone else’s. That keeps the bedtime question simple: did I drink today, yes or no. Decide before day one whether alcohol-free beer and wine count as alcohol-free for you, and keep that choice for the whole plan. For some people they fill the glass and the evening is fine. For others they keep the ritual so intact that the real thing follows.

A mogul day has a limit you pick. A drinking day isn’t a free-for-all that makes up for the days around it. Choose a number of drinks and hold it. The Slope app lets you set an optional personal limit and log against it. Count in standard drinks, not glasses: NIAAA’s standard drink guide defines one as 14 grams of pure alcohol, the amount in about 12 ounces of regular beer, 5 ounces of table wine or a 1.5-ounce shot of 80-proof spirits. The same page notes those amounts may not match the pours people actually get. A large glass of wine at a restaurant or a tall can of strong beer can hold more than one. Measuring once at home is worth the minute it takes.

What makes an alcohol-free day hard

Usually not the drink itself. It’s the slot the drink lived in: shoes off after work, the pour while dinner cooks, the second hour of a Friday with friends, the glass that marks the start of the weekend. On an off day that slot is still on the calendar with nothing in it, and filling it takes some deliberate effort, most of all on the evenings you’re tired.

The social part is its own job. People will offer, and some will ask why. NIAAA’s advice on refusing a drink is to keep it short, clear and friendly, and to skip long explanations, which tend to open a debate. “No thanks, I’m not drinking tonight” is a complete sentence. Having an alcohol-free drink already in your hand answers the question before anyone asks it.

Urges come and go. NIAAA’s page on handling urges to drink describes them as short-lived and predictable, and suggests letting one crest and pass like a wave rather than arguing with it. That is what the app’s Ride It Out exercise is for.

Cues and what to pour instead

The same NIAAA page splits triggers into outside ones, like people, places and times of day, and inside ones, like a mood, a thought or a physical feeling. The first week of off days will show you which of yours are loudest. Then plan around them:

  • Change what’s in the fridge. Keep little or no alcohol at home, which the Rethinking Drinking tips name directly, and keep something you like drinking there instead: sparkling water with citrus, a bitter soda, a good tea, something that goes in the glass you’d normally use.
  • Move the 6 pm moment. If the first drink comes with walking in the door, walk in and go straight back out for fifteen minutes, or start dinner first.
  • Look at the calendar ahead of time. The calculator shows which days are off days. If a dinner out or a work event lands on one, decide now what you’ll order. If you can, swap the event to a mogul day instead.
  • Tell one person. Someone who knows which days are off days can order two of the same alcohol-free drink without making a thing of it.

Detours, and when a taper isn’t enough

A drink on an off day is a detour. So is going past your limit on a mogul day. Log it honestly, keep the schedule as it was, and don’t add extra off days to make up for it. After a couple of weeks the log usually shows a pattern: the same night, the same people, the same kind of week. That is more useful than any rule. If you detour more than twice in a phase, stay in that phase longer with the app’s extend option rather than stepping up.

One pattern is worth taking seriously. NIAAA’s page on whether to cut down or quit strongly advises quitting, rather than cutting down, if you have tried to cut down and can’t stay within the limits you set. If your mogul-day limit keeps giving way, or the off days keep becoming drinking days whatever you try, that isn’t a reason for a stricter schedule. It’s a reason to talk to a doctor about what would help.

Who this is for

Adults of legal drinking age who drink a few times a week, aren’t physically dependent on alcohol, and want more alcohol-free days without deciding today whether they’ll quit for good. It is not for anyone the box above describes. The same NIAAA page advises quitting rather than cutting down if you are or might be pregnant, take a medicine that interacts with alcohol, or have a health condition that drinking causes or makes worse; talk to a clinician in any of those cases.
