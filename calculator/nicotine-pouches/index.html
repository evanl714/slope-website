Habit taper calculator / Nicotine pouches

Plan a gradual nicotine pouch taper.

A pouch sits under your lip where nobody sees it, so it can stay there most of the day without a single decision. Slope puts the decisions back: pouch-free days on a calendar, with more room between pouch days each phase.

The default plan assumes you use pouches every day. It runs 60 days at a starting ratio of 1:2, so for the first nine days you have two pouch days and then one pouch-free day. Every week or so the ratio moves: one on and one off, then two off per pouch day, then three, four and five. By the final two weeks, a pouch day comes around roughly once a week, and the plan ends on four pouch-free days in a row. If you already use fewer than one can a day, a shorter plan may suit you better.

Read this first. Nobody has tested tapering against quitting outright for nicotine pouches. The closest evidence comes from cigarettes, where a Cochrane review found that cutting down first and stopping all at once led to similar quit rates. A gradual plan is not the stronger route, so if you can choose a date and stop, that is a fine choice. This calculator is for people who would rather build up to it. Whichever you pick, get help with it: nicotine replacement such as gum, lozenges or a patch, free coaching from a quitline (in the US, 1-800-QUIT-NOW), or a clinician or pharmacist. The longer version is in tapering vs. cold turkey.

What counts as a pouch-free day

Counting pouches rarely holds. One goes in, you forget it is there, and the next one replaces it before you notice the first is spent. A day is easier to keep track of. On an off day there are no nicotine pouches from waking to sleep, and nothing standing in for them: no dip, no snus, no vape, no borrowed cigarette. At bedtime the question has one answer.

Two edges to settle before day one:

  • Nicotine replacement. If a pharmacist, clinician or quitline coach has helped you set up gum, lozenges or a patch, using them as directed is part of the plan and doesn’t turn an off day into a detour. Buying a different brand of pouch and calling it replacement does.
  • Nicotine-free pouches. Some people keep something under the lip on off days with a pouch that has no nicotine in it. For some it takes the edge off the physical habit. For others it keeps the reflex warm. Try it in the first phase and decide honestly which one you are.

Step the strength down on pouch days

Pouches come in several strengths. The first ones the FDA authorized for sale were 3 mg and 6 mg, and the CDC notes that higher strengths have been growing faster in sales. So the calculator’s day schedule is only half of what you can change. The other half is the can you buy.

Keep mogul days as ordinary pouch days, but move down one strength every phase or two. If you start on a high strength, drop to the middle one around the 1:1 phase and to the lowest by 3:1. Each step means the next off day starts from a smaller daily amount. Buy a single can at the new strength rather than stocking up on the old one, so the step down is already in your pocket when the day arrives.

The hard part is the lip, not the lunch break

Cigarettes interrupt a day. You stand up, go outside, come back. A pouch doesn’t interrupt anything. It rides along through meetings, a spreadsheet, the drive home, a game on the couch, and that is why an off day can take so much effort. There is no break to skip. There is a whole day with an empty spot under your lip that your tongue keeps checking.

Withdrawal sits on top of that. According to Smokefree.gov, it usually means cravings, restlessness, irritability, trouble concentrating, poor sleep and a low or anxious mood. It is strongest over the first days to weeks and weakens the longer you go. Early in this plan, each off day starts that curve over again, so expect the first phase to ask the most of you, off day after off day.

Desk cues and what to put in their place

Because pouch use is quiet, its cues are quiet too. Starting a task, getting stuck on one, finishing one. Opening the laptop. Sitting in the car. The first few off days will show you which of these reach for the can. Work on those, and let the rest be.

  • Move the can. On off days, the can doesn’t go in your pocket, your bag, the car console or the desk drawer. If you have to leave the room to get one, you have time to decide.
  • Fill the spot. Sugar-free gum, mints, sunflower seeds in the shell, a toothpick, cold water through a straw. The aim is to give your mouth something to do during the long stretches at a desk.
  • Mark the start of work. If the first pouch comes with the first email, replace it with a different small ritual: fill a water bottle, write the day’s list by hand, stretch for a minute.
  • Watch the reorder. Subscriptions and bulk rolls mean you never run out, which means you never have to choose. Pause the auto-ship while you are on the plan.
  • Ride out the wave. Cravings build, peak and fall. The Slope app’s Ride It Out exercise is there for those few minutes.

Detours

A pouch on an off day is a detour. Take it out, log it, and keep the next mogul day on the date the calendar gave it. Don’t add extra off days to make up for it. The log is the useful part: after a couple of weeks it usually shows the same one or two moments, like the afternoon slump or a long drive, and those are worth planning around by name.

If you detour more than twice in a phase, use the app’s extend option and stay at that ratio longer. If the plan stalls in the same place more than once, that’s a good moment to call a quitline. The CDC says quitline coaches can help you build a plan and may be able to provide medication for free.

Who this is for

Adults who use nicotine pouches every day and want to stop, with a steady run-up instead of a single cold start. The CDC is clear that there are no safe tobacco products, pouches included. If you’re under 21, pregnant, have a heart condition, or use pouches alongside cigarettes, a vape or other substances, talk to a clinician before you set a plan. Slope is a habit-planning tool, not medical advice or a cessation treatment.
