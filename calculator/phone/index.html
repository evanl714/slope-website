Habit taper calculator / Phone

Plan a gradual phone taper.

You can’t put a phone away for a whole day and still get to work, pay for lunch or answer your mother. So Slope schedules days with one firm rule about the phone, and adds more of them each phase.

The default plan runs 30 days at a 2:1 ratio. You keep your rule for two days, then have one ordinary day, and the stretches between ordinary days grow as the phases change: three rule days, then four, then five in a row near the finish. Across the month that works out to 23 days on your rule and 7 without it. If your phone habit is years deep, try 60 days instead of 30.

Pick one rule and make it an off day

This page is about the device as a whole: the checking, the unlocking, the picking up for no reason while you wait for the kettle. If one kind of app is the real problem, the social media calculator is a closer fit. Here, an off day is any day you keep a single phone rule you chose in advance. At bedtime you should be able to answer “did I keep it?” with a plain yes or no. Three rules that pass that test:

  • A cutoff hour. After, say, 9 pm the phone is for calls and messages from actual people, and nothing else. No browsing, no videos, no “just checking.” Pick the hour and write it down.
  • The phone sleeps somewhere else. It charges overnight in the kitchen or the hall, not next to the bed. A cheap alarm clock does the one job that kept it on the nightstand.
  • A phone-free first hour. Nothing on the screen until you’ve been up for an hour, or until you’ve left the house. The morning starts before the inbox does.

Keep a short list of what’s allowed on off days, because some of the phone is genuinely needed: calls, maps, a two-factor code, a boarding pass. Anything not on that list counts. Choose one rule for the whole plan. Changing the rule halfway makes the log hard to read.

Mogul days are ordinary days with the phone. There’s nothing to earn and nothing to make up for. They are also a good time to notice how you use it, which makes the next off day easier to plan.

Why the evening rules are worth trying first

Two of the three rules above are about the bedroom, and that’s on purpose. Sleep tips from the CDC’s workplace health institute on improving sleep say to keep phone and computer screens away from your eyes in the wind-down time before bed, and not to let phones disturb your sleep. A large study of more than 120,000 US adults found that people who used screens before bed every night went to bed later and slept less over the week than people who didn’t. That is an association, not proof that the phone caused it, but it’s a reasonable place to start if tired mornings are part of why you’re here.

What actually makes an off day hard

It isn’t the lack of information. It’s the gaps. Most phone use happens in thirty-second pockets: the elevator, the checkout line, the ad break, the moment a conversation goes quiet. On an off day those pockets are still there and your hand still goes to your pocket. Expect that to happen a lot at first, and expect keeping the rule to take real effort in the evenings, when you have the least left for it.

The other hard part is that the phone is how people reach you. Putting it down can feel like being unreachable, even when nobody is trying. Telling the people who matter what your rule is, and that calls still get through, takes most of the edge off.

Cues and what to put in their place

A phone habit runs on cues more than on content. A few changes that make your rule easier to keep:

  • Turn on a Focus or Downtime schedule that matches your cutoff hour, and allow calls from your favorites through it.
  • Mute everything that isn’t a person. Badges, shopping apps, news alerts. Each one is a small invitation to pick the phone up.
  • Give your hands a job. A paper book on the couch, a crossword, a notebook, a podcast started before the cutoff that plays from a speaker.
  • Move the charger, not just the phone. If the charging cable lives in the kitchen, the phone ends up there without a nightly argument.

When the pull is strong, the app’s Ride It Out exercise gives you something to do for the minute or two until it passes.

Detours

Scrolling past your cutoff, or finding the phone back on the nightstand, is a detour. Log it, put the phone back where your rule says it goes, and keep the schedule as it was. You don’t owe the plan an extra off day. Look at the log after a week or two: detours tend to cluster around the same trigger, like a late work message or a night alone. If you have more than two in a phase, extend that phase in the app before stepping up to the next ratio.

Who this is for

Adults who want to use their phone less without pretending they can live without one. If your phone use is tied to anxiety, depression, or a compulsion that is getting in the way of work, sleep or relationships, a therapist or doctor is a better place to start, and you can still use a plan alongside that.
