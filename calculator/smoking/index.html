Habit taper calculator / Smoking

Plan a gradual smoking taper.

Cutting down on cigarettes works best when it has an end date. Slope builds the road to one: smoke-free days that grow longer each phase until the last day of the plan becomes your quit day.

The calculator opens at a 1:2 ratio over 60 days, set up for someone who smokes every day. You start with two smoking days and then one smoke-free day, on repeat for about nine days. After that the ratio moves each week or so: one on and one off, then two off per smoking day, then three, and so on. Across the two months that comes to 41 smoke-free days and 19 smoking days, and the plan closes on a run of smoke-free days that leads straight into your quit date. If you already have a date in mind, set the finish date to match it.

Read this first. Cutting down before you quit is not the stronger option. A Cochrane review of 22 studies found that people who reduced first and people who stopped all at once ended up quitting at about the same rate, and in a large 2016 trial, stopping abruptly did better. So if you can pick a quit date and stop on it, do that. This schedule is for people who have tried that and want a slower way in. Either way, don’t do it alone: nicotine replacement can more than double your chances, and in the US 1-800-QUIT-NOW connects you to free coaching. A clinician or pharmacist can help you choose medication. More on the trade-off in tapering vs. cold turkey.

A quit date with a runway

Most people who say they’re cutting down count cigarettes: twenty a day, then fifteen, then ten. The count is hard to hold, because every cigarette you skip feels like one you can have later. Slope counts days instead. An off day means no cigarettes from the moment you wake to the moment you sleep. Not one, not a drag of someone else’s, and no swapping in a cigar or a borrowed vape. The answer at the end of the day is yes or no, and that makes it much harder to argue with.

Set the finish date in the calculator to the day you intend to quit. The phases then do the preparation for you. Early on, an off day is a single day between smoking days. By the last few weeks, you are going four and five days at a time without a cigarette. The day after your final mogul day is quit day, and by then you’ve already done most of what it asks many times.

That is the case for doing it this way. The Cochrane review found reducing first works about as well as stopping outright, not better, and it found reduction went better for people who used medication along the way. So treat nicotine replacement as part of the plan, not a backup:

  • Off days are for nicotine replacement. The review’s strongest evidence was for fast-acting forms like gum and lozenges, used while cutting down. Keep them with you on off days, and follow the package directions.
  • Mogul days are for smoking as usual. You don’t need to trim those days too. The spacing is the taper.
  • Plan past quit day. Nicotine replacement and quitline coaching keep helping in the weeks after your last cigarette. A pharmacist can help you decide on a patch alongside gum or lozenges.

What makes a smoke-free day hard

Two things arrive at once on an off day. The first is withdrawal. According to Smokefree.gov, it usually means cravings, irritability, restlessness, trouble concentrating, poor sleep, and sadness or anxiety, and it is strongest in the first days to weeks and fades the longer you go. In the first phase of this plan a smoke-free day sits between two smoking days, so you meet the front edge of that over and over. It takes real effort each time, and nobody expects the tenth one to be the same as the first.

The second is the day itself. Cigarettes are built into the shape of a smoker’s day: the first one with coffee, the one on the way to work, the break outside with the same two coworkers, after lunch, in the car, the last one on the back step before bed. On an off day each of those moments still happens. It just has a gap in the middle where the cigarette was.

Cues and what goes in the gap

Your first few off days will show you which moments pull hardest. Plan for those first, and leave the quiet ones alone.

  • Move the morning. If the first cigarette comes with coffee on the porch, have the coffee somewhere else on off days, or switch to tea for those mornings only.
  • Keep the break, drop the smoke. Breaks are worth keeping. Go for a walk instead of to the smoking spot, and let the coworkers you usually smoke with know which days are off days.
  • Give your mouth and hands a job. Gum, hard candy, a toothpick, a bottle of water, a pen to turn over. Smokefree.gov’s craving tips include slow, deep breaths and a few flights of stairs as well.
  • Clear the car. Empty the ashtray and keep the pack out of the glovebox on off days. The drive is one of the hardest cues because your hands already know the routine.
  • Ride out the wave. A craving builds, peaks and passes. The Slope app’s Ride It Out exercise walks you through that stretch.

Detours

A cigarette on an off day is a detour. Log it, keep the next mogul day where the calendar put it, and don’t add extra off days as a penalty. One cigarette doesn’t undo the phase. The log is more useful than a tally, because a few weeks of it will usually point at one or two moments that keep winning: the Friday drinks, the drive home, a certain person. Plan around those, or move them onto mogul days.

If you detour more than twice in a phase, stay in that phase longer with the app’s extend option. If the plan keeps stalling at the same ratio, that is a good time to call a quitline or talk to a clinician about medication rather than pushing on by yourself. The CDC notes that quitline coaches can build a plan with you and may be able to send you free medication.

Who this is for

Adults who smoke cigarettes every day, want to quit, and want a structured runway to a quit date instead of a single cold start. If you can stop outright, that is the better bet. If you are pregnant, have a heart condition, take regular medication or also use other tobacco products, talk to a clinician before you set a plan. Slope is a habit-planning tool, not medical advice or a cessation treatment.
