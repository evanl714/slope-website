Habit taper calculator / Food delivery

Plan a gradual food delivery taper.

Delivery apps are built so that ordering is the path of least resistance at the end of a long day. Slope gives you nights that are already decided: no-order days, spread a little further apart each phase.

The calculator loads a 2:1 ratio over 45 days. You go two days without placing a delivery order, then have one day when ordering is fine, and each phase stretches the gap until you reach six no-order days between orders. Over the whole 45 days that comes to nine delivery days. If you order a few times a week rather than most nights, try 3:1 or a longer plan. If two nights running feels like a stretch, begin at 1:1.

Decide what an off day rules out

“Order less” turns into a negotiation every evening, and the tired version of you negotiates badly. An off day settles it in advance: no delivery order placed, from any app, for any meal. At the end of the day the answer is yes or no. A few edge cases come up often enough to decide before you start:

  • Pickup. Driving to collect a takeout order you placed in an app still counts as an order for most people, since the tap is the habit. Some people allow calling a restaurant and walking over. Pick one rule and write it down.
  • Someone else orders. If a partner or roommate orders for the house, that isn’t your detour. If you ask them to, it probably is. Tell them which days are your off days so the question comes up less.
  • Grocery delivery. A weekly grocery drop is usually part of the plan, not the problem. Leave it out unless the grocery app has become where the late-night orders happen now.
  • Work lunches. Team orders you don’t pay for and didn’t start can sit outside the rule. Decide that once, not in the moment.

On mogul days, order what you like from where you like. Some people save their favorite place for these days, which makes them worth waiting for and makes the off days feel less like going without.

The 9 pm order

For most people the difficult order is not the planned Friday night one. It’s the weekday evening when work ran late, the kitchen feels far away, and the app is one thumb away with a saved card and a reorder button. That moment is less about wanting a specific meal and more about not wanting to decide anything else today. An off day asks you to make one more decision when you have the least energy for it, and that takes real effort. Plan for it rather than hoping it won’t come.

  • Have a default dinner. One or two meals you can put together in ten minutes with no thought, kept in stock. The point isn’t cooking well. It’s having an answer ready before 9 pm asks the question.
  • Decide at lunch. On off days, pick the evening meal before the afternoon runs out. A choice made at 1 pm holds up better than one made at the end of the day.
  • Eat out instead, if you want. Sitting down at a restaurant or picking something up on the way home isn’t a delivery order. The goal is fewer app orders, not fewer meals you enjoy.
  • Ride it out. If the urge to open the app hits hard, the Slope app’s Ride It Out exercise covers the few minutes it usually takes to pass.

What an order actually costs

The price on the menu is rarely the price you pay, and that gap is part of why spending on delivery is hard to track. In a 2024 case, the Federal Trade Commission alleged that Grubhub advertised low prices and then added fees labeled as service or small-order fees, so the final total was often more than double what was advertised, and that its subscription was sold as “free” delivery while subscribers were still charged for delivery. In 2026 the FTC asked for public comment on hidden and misleading fees across food and grocery delivery platforms, including whether apps make clear when item prices differ from the restaurant’s own.

You don’t have to work out who charges what. A few changes make the cost visible again and add a pause before checkout:

  • Look at the full total, after fees and tip, before you tap. On mogul days, note it in the app’s log or a notes file. A month of totals says more than any single order.
  • Remove saved cards from the delivery apps and turn off the one-tap reorder where you can.
  • Check whether you pay for a delivery membership, and when it renews. A monthly fee makes each order feel already paid for.
  • Turn off promotional notifications. Discount pushes tend to land at dinner time, which is exactly the window you’re working on.
  • Move the apps into a folder off your home screen, or log out on off days.

Detours

An order on an off day is a detour. Log it and let the schedule carry on; the next mogul day stays where it was, and there are no extra off days to make up for it. The log is where the useful part is. After two phases you’ll likely see detours gather around particular nights: Sunday evenings, the day of a long meeting, the nights you get home after eight. That tells you where the default dinner needs to be ready. If you detour more than twice in one phase, use the app’s extend option to stay at that ratio a while longer before stepping up.

Who this is for

Adults who order delivery more often than they’d like, usually because of the money or the sense that the evenings run on autopilot, and who want a plan that leaves room for the orders they enjoy. It isn’t about what you eat. If ordering is tied to anxiety, low mood, or exhaustion that’s affecting your life more widely, or the spending has turned into debt that worries you, a therapist or a nonprofit credit counselor is a better place to start than a schedule.
