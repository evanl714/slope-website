How this taper calculator works

Most quit plans count doses: one fewer cigarette, one fewer coffee, ten fewer minutes. This calculator counts days instead. Every day on your schedule is either an off day, when you take a break from the habit, or a mogul day, when you’ve planned to allow it. Your starting ratio describes how those days alternate at the beginning. A 3:1 ratio means three off days followed by one mogul day. A 1:1 ratio alternates. If you’re starting from every day, you can begin inverted with a 1:2 or 1:3 ratio: one off day for every two or three habit days.

The calculator splits your timeline into phases. Each phase repeats its ratio for a set number of cycles, then the next phase adds one more off day to the pattern. 3:1 becomes 4:1, then 5:1, and so on until your completion date. Phase length scales with your timeline, so a longer plan spends more time at each step. The final phase ends on your completion date even if that lands partway through a cycle, so the plan always finishes when you said it would.

This is the same schedule generator used inside the Slope app. What you preview here is exactly what a fresh plan looks like there, before any logged detours or custom phases.

Why spacing days beats cutting doses

For many habits there isn’t a clean dose to cut. Scrolling, gambling urges, snacking, and pornography don’t come in milligrams. Even for habits that do, like caffeine or nicotine, dose cutting tends to drift. A “smaller” coffee becomes a large one by Thursday, and after a couple of weeks it’s hard to tell whether you’re actually tapering.

Days are easier to keep honest. An off day is a yes-or-no question, and you can answer it at bedtime. Spacing also gives you something dose cutting doesn’t: practice at the thing you’re ultimately trying to do, which is getting through a whole day without the habit. Each phase makes those stretches a little longer. By the time you reach your quit date, a week without the habit is something you’ve already done several times, not a leap.

For substances, spacing works well alongside a smaller amount. Keeping mogul days modest while adding off days between them is a reasonable way to soften the transition. Our guide to tapering off caffeine without headaches walks through that combination for coffee and energy drinks.

One honest caveat. Gradual isn’t automatically better. In a large randomized trial, smokers who quit abruptly with support did slightly better than smokers who reduced first. And for alcohol, benzodiazepines, or opioids, withdrawal can be medically dangerous and needs a clinician, not an app. We cover the evidence in tapering vs. cold turkey.

What a 14-day plan vs. a 90-day plan looks like

The timeline you choose changes the plan more than the ratio does. Here’s how a 3:1 start plays out across three common timelines.

TimelinePhasesRatiosMogul daysOff days
14 days23:1 → 4:1311
30 days33:1 → 5:1624
90 days73:1 → 9:11476

A 14-day plan is a sprint. It only has room for two phases, so it works best for a habit you’ve already loosened, where you mostly want a firm end date and a few planned days on the way there. Fourteen days is also the minimum the app allows.

A 90-day plan is a slope. By the middle of it you’re spending a week between mogul days, and the last phase stretches to nine off days at a time. That suits a daily, long-standing habit, where the point is to make each step small enough that you barely notice the ground changing. If you’re starting from every day, a 90-day plan with a 1:3 start gives you eight phases and still gets you to 6:1 by the end.

Something in between, around 30 to 60 days, is where most people land. Use the calendar above to check the plan against real life. If a phase change lands on a weekend or a trip that worries you, shift the completion date a few days and look again.

What to do on a detour

A detour is the habit showing up on a scheduled off day. It will happen, and the plan is built to absorb it. A few things help:

  • Log it the same day. In the app, recording a detour keeps the record honest and shows you the pattern later. A detour you don’t write down turns into a story about failure instead of a data point.
  • Don’t compensate. Adding extra off days to “make up for it” usually produces a harder week and a second detour. Keep the next mogul day where it is.
  • Give the phase more time if you need to. The app lets you repeat your current phase instead of moving on to the next ratio. Two or three detours in one phase is a sign the step was too big, not that you’re done.
  • Look for the shape. Detours cluster: Friday evenings, the day after a bad night’s sleep, a particular person. Once you can name the trigger, you can plan the next mogul day around it instead of being ambushed.

This calculator previews a fresh plan. Logged detours and phase extensions change the schedule inside the app, which is why the same start and end dates can look different there once you’re a few weeks in.

Who this is for

Slope is a habit-planning tool, not medical advice. It’s a good fit for behavioral habits and for medically uncomplicated substances like caffeine. If reducing a substance could cause withdrawal, or you’ve been advised to taper with medical supervision, talk to a clinician before making a plan. Custom phases and logged detours are available in the app, and everything you enter here stays in your browser.
