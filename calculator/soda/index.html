Habit taper calculator / Soda

Plan a gradual soda taper.

The can with lunch, the bottle in the car, the fridge at work that’s always stocked. Slope schedules soda-free days and moves them a little further apart each phase.

The calculator opens at 1:1 over 45 days: one soda-free day, then one day you drink soda the way you do now, back and forth for the first week. Each phase after that adds one more soda-free day to the run, so by the last two weeks you’re at five in a row between soda days, and the plan closes on a soda-free stretch. The gentle start is deliberate. Most soda habits are daily and many carry caffeine, so the first phase is where the work is. Change either setting if 1:1 is too much or too little.

What a soda-free day means

“Drink less soda” tends to mean one fewer can on a good day and two more on a long one. A soda-free day doesn’t leave room for that kind of arithmetic. At bedtime you can answer it in a word. Set the edges before you start and write them down, because they’re much harder to decide at 3 p.m. in front of a vending machine:

  • Every kind of soda counts. Cola, lemon-lime, root beer, ginger ale, the no-sugar versions of all of them, and the fountain cup that comes with a combo. If one variety is exempt, the habit moves into it.
  • Decide about the neighbors. Energy drinks, sweet tea and sparkling juice sit right next to soda in the cooler. Put them on the list or leave them off, but decide once. If caffeine is the bigger habit for you, the caffeine calculator may be the better starting point.
  • Plain sparkling water is allowed. Unflavored or lightly flavored seltzer with nothing sweet in it is the most useful substitute on this page. Keeping it off the list is what makes the rest of the list workable.

Mogul days are regular soda days. Drink what you normally drink. As the phases move on, you can shrink the mogul days too if you want: a can instead of a 20-ounce bottle, a small fountain drink instead of a large. That’s optional. The spacing does most of the work.

The caffeine part

A lot of soda has caffeine in it, and people who drink it daily often don’t count it the way they’d count coffee. The FDA puts a 12-ounce caffeinated soft drink at 23 to 83 milligrams, depending on the brand, and advises that anyone cutting back on regular caffeine do it gradually: withdrawal isn’t considered dangerous, but it can be unpleasant. Three or four cans across a workday adds up to a real dose.

If your soda is caffeinated, the first off days may bring a headache, tiredness, fog, or a short temper. A review of caffeine withdrawal studies found that symptoms usually begin 12 to 24 hours after the last dose, peak between about 20 and 51 hours, and last from two to nine days, and that stopping doses as low as 100 mg a day was enough to produce them. On a 1:1 schedule you never go more than a day without caffeine in the first phase, which keeps much of that at bay. The longer runs come later, after your body has had a few weeks of practice.

If you also drink coffee or tea, keep those steady while you taper soda. Changing everything at once makes it hard to tell which change is causing the headache.

What makes an off day hard

For many people the caffeine is the smaller piece. The harder part is that soda is attached to specific moments, and on an off day those moments still arrive with nothing in hand. It takes effort, and it tends to land in a few predictable places:

  • With a meal. Lunch without the can can feel unfinished. Order or pack the replacement drink ahead of time, so the choice is already made when you sit down.
  • The afternoon slump. Around 2 or 3 p.m., the soda is a break, a walk to the fridge and a small reward in one. Keep the walk and the break. Change what you bring back.
  • Driving and errands. Gas station stops and drive-throughs are built around the fountain cup. On off days, bring a drink from home and put it in the cup holder before you leave.
  • Whatever’s in the fridge. A stocked fridge makes every off day a string of small refusals. Buying fewer cans at once, or keeping them out of sight, turns that into one decision at the store.

For substitutes, the ones that tend to stick share something with soda: cold, fizzy, a little flavor. Seltzer with a squeeze of lime or a splash of juice, unsweetened iced tea, or cold water from a bottle you actually like. If a craving hits hard, the Slope app’s Ride It Out exercise walks you through the next few minutes.

Detours

A soda on an off day is a detour. Log it and keep going. The next mogul day stays where the schedule put it, and you don’t owe the plan an extra off day to make up for it. The log is more useful than the count: if the detours cluster on one weekday, or always happen at the same drive-through, that’s a cue you can plan around. If you detour more than twice in a phase, stay in that phase longer with the app’s extend option before moving to the next ratio.

Who this is for

Adults who drink soda most days and want to drink it less often, or stop, without a sudden cold start. If soda is part of how you’re managing a health condition or a medication schedule, or you’re pregnant and cutting back on caffeine, check with a clinician about the pace. If the habit is bound up with anxiety, depression, or a compulsion that’s affecting your life more broadly, a therapist or doctor is a better first stop.
